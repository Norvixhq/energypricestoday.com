Editor’s Note — What we’re watching

The Beijing summit produced the clearest U.S.–China alignment yet on Hormuz: both sides agreed the strait must remain open and Iran cannot have a nuclear weapon, and Xi reportedly offered to help broker peace. The harder Trump warning to Iran — reach a deal or face “annihilation” — came hours later. The two together establish the framework for what happens next: a 20-year nuclear moratorium offer brokered by Pakistan, or a return to military action. Markets are pricing the uncertainty — WTI is on track for a roughly 10% weekly gain.

EnergyPricesToday Editorial · May 15, 2026

The Trump-Xi summit produced an alignment of headline positions, if not detailed enforcement commitments. Both leaders agreed in the White House readout that the Strait of Hormuz must remain open and that Iran cannot have a nuclear weapon. Xi opposed both the militarization of the strait and any effort to charge a transit toll, and expressed interest in purchasing more American crude over time. The Chinese readout was notably quieter on Iran by name — Xinhua said only that the two leaders “exchanged views” on the Middle East situation.

Hours after the summit concluded, Trump warned Iran of “annihilation” if a deal remains out of reach. Speaking to reporters, Trump said: “We may have to do a little cleanup work because we had a month-long ceasefire.” The administration is reportedly offering Iran a 20-year verified moratorium on its nuclear program, surrender of all highly enriched uranium, and free commercial traffic through Hormuz as conditions to end hostilities. Pakistan has been acting as intermediary. Treasury Secretary Bessent told CNBC the same day: “China has a much bigger interest in reopening the strait than the U.S. does.”

Beneath the diplomacy, the supply picture remains structurally tight. The IEA reports crude and fuel flows through Hormuz fell about 4 million barrels per day in March and April, with undersupply likely through October even if the conflict ends sooner. The EIA confirmed U.S. commercial crude inventories fell 4.3 million barrels for the week ending May 8. The UAE’s departure from OPEC, effective May 1, cut OPEC’s 2027 spare capacity forecast to 2.5M bpd from 3.8M. Saudi Arabia informed OPEC its output has fallen to the lowest level since 1990.
