America barely uses Middle East oil. So why did gas prices rise?

Staff May 14, 2026 5 min read

U.S. crude oil imports from the Middle East have fallen dramatically over the past decade, shrinking to roughly 5–8% of total U.S. oil supply. Yet when tensions in the region escalate—whether from geopolitical conflict, shipping disruptions, or OPEC+ production decisions—American fuel prices often rise sharply anyway. This counterintuitive dynamic reflects how global oil markets function: crude is priced as a fungible commodity, and disruptions anywhere affect prices everywhere.

The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical chokepoint through which roughly 20–25% of the world’s seaborne oil passes daily. When supply risks emerge in that region, traders immediately price in scarcity premiums across Brent and WTI futures, regardless of whether the affected barrels were bound for U.S. refineries. A tanker attack or production outage in the Gulf can push global crude prices higher within hours, cascading into U.S. pump prices within days.

OPEC+ production decisions also affect American consumers indirectly through the global benchmark. Saudi Arabia, Iraq, and other Middle Eastern members control enough spare capacity to move markets materially. When these producers cut output or signal tighter policy, it lifts the cost floor for all crude worldwide—including the lighter, tighter supplies that feed American refineries.

The disconnect between U.S. import share and price sensitivity underscores a fundamental truth: domestically produced oil and strategic reserves matter less than global supply-demand equilibrium. Even refineries processing Canadian or Texan crude face elevated feedstock costs when Middle East uncertainties tighten global markets. Until the U.S. becomes energy-isolated from world pricing dynamics, regional shocks will continue to ripple through American gas stations.
