America barely uses Middle East oil. So why did gas prices rise?

Staff May 2, 2026 5 min read

U.S. crude imports from the Middle East have fallen sharply over the past decade, dropping from roughly 2 million barrels per day in 2010 to under 500,000 bpd today. Yet pump prices at American gas stations remain tethered to global oil markets, where Middle Eastern supply disruptions still matter enormously. This disconnect puzzles many consumers who assume domestic energy independence means insulation from overseas shocks.

The reason is straightforward: oil is a globally traded commodity, and prices are set on the margin. Even if the U.S. produces more crude than it consumes, refineries and traders buy and sell at world prices determined by OPEC+ decisions, geopolitical tensions, and supply disruptions anywhere that affects the marginal barrel. A production cut by Saudi Arabia or a shipping chokepoint in the Strait of Hormuz tightens global supply, raising prices everywhere — including at American pumps.

Refining margins and regional logistics also play a role. U.S. refineries process a mix of domestic, Canadian, and international crudes, optimizing for yields and margins. When Middle Eastern production dips or shipping costs spike, global crude prices rise across the board, and refiners pass those costs forward. Additionally, some U.S. refineries are configured specifically for heavier Middle Eastern crudes, making them less flexible during disruptions.

The broader lesson is that energy markets operate on global fundamentals, not national production alone. The U.S. energy sector has gained real autonomy through shale and reduced import dependence, but it cannot escape the laws of global commodity pricing. Until oil trading becomes regionalized — which no major economy has achieved — American consumers will continue to feel the ripple effects of Middle Eastern geopolitics, regardless of how little crude the country actually imports.
