America barely uses Middle East oil. So why did gas prices rise?

Staff May 4, 2026 5 min read

U.S. crude oil imports from the Middle East have fallen to historically low levels, driven by domestic shale production and strategic reserves management. Yet retail gasoline prices remain sensitive to global crude benchmarks, particularly Brent, which reflects supply and demand dynamics across all major producing regions. When Middle East tensions or OPEC+ production decisions move Brent futures, American refiners pay more for the marginal barrel they source—and that cost typically flows through to the pump.

The disconnect stems from how global oil markets work. Even if U.S. refineries process mostly domestic crude, they operate within a world market where Brent pricing sets the cost floor for uncontracted barrels. A supply disruption in the Middle East tightens the global market, raises Brent, and refiners adjust their feedstock costs accordingly. American consumers feel that pressure regardless of where the oil physically originates.

Additionally, refined products like gasoline and diesel are themselves traded globally. U.S. refineries export significant volumes of finished fuel, and import some, linking domestic prices to international shipping routes and global refinery margins. A crisis affecting Middle Eastern output can tighten refined product availability worldwide, affecting prices at American terminals.

The upshot: insulation from Middle East oil is incomplete. The U.S. energy sector benefits from lower import dependence, but crude is still a globally priced commodity. Supply shocks thousands of miles away influence pump prices through interconnected futures markets, refining economics, and product trade—a reality that persists even as direct imports decline.
