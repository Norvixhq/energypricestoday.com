America barely uses Middle East oil. So why did gas prices rise?

Staff May 5, 2026 5 min read

U.S. crude oil imports from the Middle East have declined sharply over the past decade, falling from roughly 1.5 million barrels per day in 2008 to less than 500,000 bpd in recent years. Domestic shale production, Canadian imports, and diversified sourcing from Latin America and West Africa have reduced American reliance on Persian Gulf oil to historic lows. Yet gasoline prices at the pump remain sensitive to global crude benchmarks, even when direct Middle Eastern supply plays a minor role in U.S. inventories.

Global oil markets operate as a unified system where pricing is set by worldwide supply and demand, not by regional availability alone. Brent Crude, the international benchmark priced in London, reflects production decisions across OPEC+, geopolitical tensions, and demand signals from Europe, Asia, and beyond. When Middle Eastern supply contracts or geopolitical risks spike in that region, Brent prices rise across the board — and U.S. refiners buying on the global market pass those costs downstream to consumers.

Refining networks further complicate the picture: while American refineries process less Middle Eastern crude, they remain integrated into global energy markets through product swaps, trading, and interconnected logistics. A supply disruption in the Gulf can ripple through Atlantic Basin shipping routes, affect European refinery margins, and ultimately influence the cost of gasoline components or finished products that do reach U.S. terminals. The U.S. energy market is therefore exposed to Middle Eastern shocks even when direct crude imports are minimal.

For consumers, the lesson is straightforward: domestic oil independence does not insulate the U.S. from global price pressures. As long as American refineries and traders operate in a world market, and as long as crude benchmarks respond to supply changes anywhere on Earth, Middle Eastern instability or OPEC+ decisions will continue to affect what Americans pay at the pump. Energy security involves not just where oil comes from, but how tightly linked global markets remain.
