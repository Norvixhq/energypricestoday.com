America barely uses Middle East oil. So why did gas prices rise?

Staff May 13, 2026 5 min read

U.S. crude oil imports from the Middle East have fallen to historic lows, yet domestic fuel prices remain sensitive to geopolitical events in the region. This apparent disconnect reflects how global oil markets work: prices are set on world exchanges, where crude from all sources competes. Even if American refineries process mostly domestic shale, the global benchmark price—whether Brent or WTI—responds to supply disruptions anywhere, and U.S. consumers pay that world price at the pump.

The Middle East still accounts for roughly one-third of global oil production, and any real or perceived threat to that output ripples across markets instantly. A flare-up in the Strait of Hormuz, OPEC+ production cuts, or tensions in major producers like Saudi Arabia or Iraq can tighten global supply faster than North American output can compensate. Traders price in this risk immediately, lifting crude futures and, within days, retail gasoline and diesel.

Refinery capacity also plays a hidden role. U.S. refineries are optimized to process specific crude types—many rely on blends that include Middle Eastern barrels for cost and operational efficiency. If Middle East supply tightens, refiners may need to source costlier alternatives or adjust operations, pushing up the cost of finished fuel. This adds another layer of price transmission from a region America no longer depends on for import volume.

The bottom line: energy markets are global, and prices reflect worldwide supply and demand. American energy independence in crude doesn’t isolate U.S. consumers from Middle Eastern shocks. As long as the region produces a significant share of world oil and geopolitical risk exists there, U.S. gas pumps will respond to events thousands of miles away.
