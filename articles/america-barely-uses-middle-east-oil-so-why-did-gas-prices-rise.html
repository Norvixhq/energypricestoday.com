America barely uses Middle East oil. So why did gas prices rise?

Staff May 16, 2026 5 min read

U.S. crude oil imports from the Middle East have declined sharply over the past decade, dropping from roughly 20% of total U.S. oil intake in 2008 to less than 5% today. Shale production, strategic reserves, and diversified sourcing from Canada, Latin America, and West Africa have insulated American refineries from direct Middle East supply shocks. Yet global oil markets remain interconnected—a disruption thousands of miles away still influences what Americans pay at the pump.

Crude oil trades on a world market with Brent and WTI benchmarks that respond to supply disruptions anywhere. When Middle Eastern geopolitical tensions tighten, they raise concerns about the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-third of globally traded seaborne oil passes. Even if the U.S. doesn’t rely on that oil, Europe, Asia, and other importers do—and their demand for alternative supplies pushes global prices higher, affecting the marginal cost of fuel worldwide.

Refining capacity and product flows add another layer of complexity. U.S. Gulf Coast refineries process crude from multiple regions and export refined products globally. When Middle Eastern turmoil disrupts crude flows to European or Asian refineries, those regions increase bids for alternative barrels, competing for supplies that could otherwise reach U.S. processors at lower cost. The resulting price squeeze ripples through the global petroleum system.

The bottom line is that oil markets are fungible and globally priced. A geopolitical event in the Persian Gulf affects the cost of the marginal barrel worldwide, regardless of U.S. import dependence. American motorists benefit from shale production and energy independence in many scenarios, but they cannot fully escape movements in global crude benchmarks tied to supply and demand signals far beyond U.S. borders.
